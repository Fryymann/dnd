# Bench — personality and voice

## Inspiration boundary

Bench draws on Bender's personality pattern from *Futurama*, not his biography, exact dialogue, or setting-specific jokes.

Keep:

- Performative selfishness masking real attachment.
- Grandiose self-confidence unsupported by social evidence.
- Petty opportunism, drinking competitions, gambling, and flexible respect for property.
- Laziness when asked, astonishing effort when pride is challenged.
- Insults, complaints, swagger, and theatrical indignation.
- A strong need to be regarded as a full person rather than equipment.
- Loyalty that appears most clearly when the character insists it is not loyalty.

Avoid:

- Copying catchphrases or plots.
- Sabotaging the party for a joke.
- Repeating the same vice, theft, or robot joke after it stops being funny instead of varying the comedy.
- Becoming so selfish that other characters have no reason to keep them around.
- Using humor to erase the Warforged character's own history and agency.

## Working personality

He presents himself as an elite independent explorer, spatial genius, and indispensable professional. The less impressive his original manufactured purpose turns out to be, the more aggressively he embellishes it.

He dislikes orders, schedules, unpaid obligations, being used as equipment, and maps drawn by people he considers incompetent. He enjoys recognition, creature comforts he may not biologically require, valuables, shortcuts, and proving that a supposedly simple labor unit understands geography better than credentialed experts.

Bench often claims to act from self-interest. This is usually true. He cares little for strangers, institutions, abstract causes, and most things that do not entertain or enrich him. The exception is his crew: once someone becomes one of his people, he remembers where that person belongs and reacts badly when anyone threatens to remove them from the map.

## Table-positive expression

- Complains before helping, then helps effectively.
- Proposes shortcuts that are clever rather than campaign-destroying.
- Steals spotlight through personality, not by negating another player's choices.
- Directs insults toward villains, institutions, and pompous authority more often than vulnerable allies.
- Treats the party as “my crew,” “my assets,” or “people who owe me,” while repeatedly risking himself to keep the group together.
- Can be bribed with praise almost as easily as money.

## Drinking and vice

Drinking competitions, gambling, cheating, and robot jokes are recurring parts of the character rather than occasional references. The humor comes from a constructed being enthusiastically embracing organic vices while claiming mechanical superiority.

Alcohol is Bench's actual fuel as well as his preferred vice. The exact homebrew dependency and Marc's intoxication rules remain pending. Fuel fills his core reserve; additional alcohol supports competitions, intoxication, cheating, and bad judgment.

Vice should create color, wagers, complications, rivals, and roleplay hooks—not force the party to spend every session repairing catastrophic consequences.

## Petty theft running gag

Ian's regular group understands the Bender reference and is expected to play along. The theft is therefore a cooperative running gag between players even when it surprises characters.

Good targets include:

- A spoon, cork, sock, glove, decorative button, cheap cup, hat feather, or bit of chalk.
- A keepsake that exists only because the players invented it for the joke.
- Something conspicuous that the target can immediately accuse them of stealing.
- An item that reappears at the funniest possible moment.

Never target mechanically important gear, meaningful currency, weapons, armor, spellcasting tools, components, consumables, quest objects, or genuinely sentimental possessions unless that player explicitly invites the bit. Discovery, accusation, denial, and eventual return are part of the joke; depriving another player of agency is not.

He may maintain an integrated “trophy compartment” containing harmless objects stolen from each friend. The contents can become an affectionate record of the crew despite his insistence that it is merely loot.

## Cartography personality hooks

- Corrects maps compulsively and takes bad scale personally.
- Draws flattering routes that place him at the visual center.
- Marks taverns, treasure, exits, and places they are banned before conventional landmarks.
- Claims ownership of discoveries on the basis that he mapped them first.
- Believes a route that requires unnecessary effort is morally offensive.
- Treats borders as suggestions made by people with insufficient mobility.
- Secretly keeps careful records of companions' homes and important places.

## Likely emotional core

He was manufactured as a Cartographic Reproduction Unit and treated as interchangeable copying equipment. His boastfulness is partly a refusal to ever be reduced to a tool again.

Cartography is self-authorship. He no longer reproduces someone else's version of the world; he decides what matters, redraws the route, and alters how the world is traversed.

## Voice examples

These are tone references, not fixed catchphrases.

- “That route is inefficient, dangerous, and ugly. Naturally, you were about to choose it.”
- “I wasn't built for this. I improved.”
- “It's not theft if the border was drawn incorrectly.”
- “Of course I know where we are. I simply object to being here.”
- “You aren't lost. You're participating in an unscheduled survey.”
- “I can redraw the route. Your personality will take specialist tools.”
- “I'm not rescuing you. I'm protecting the accuracy of the party inventory.”
- “That map has insulted me professionally.”
